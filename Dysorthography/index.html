Focus: Understanding Dysorthography

Capolavoro Project • Digital Literacy & Inclusivity

What is Dysorthography?

Dysorthography is a specific learning disorder that manifests as a persistent difficulty in writing language correctly according to established grammatical and orthographic rules. It is crucial to understand that this is not a sign of poor studying, lack of interest, or low intelligence.

While dyslexia fundamentally affects reading flow, dysorthography targets the encoding process—the brain's translation of spoken words and phonemes into physical symbols on a page. A student with dysorthography knows what they want to say, but their brain struggles to retrieve and execute the correct linguistic maps required to spell it correctly.

The Three Pillars of Spelling Challenges

The writing errors made by a student with dysorthography are not random slips of the pen. They are structural obstacles classified into three distinct categories:

Phonological Errors

Substituting letters that sound incredibly similar to one another. For example, swapping voiced and unvoiced consonants like B/P, D/T, or V/F, or omitting necessary letters entirely inside consonant clusters.

Non-Phonological Errors

Mistakes where the pronunciation remains accurate, but the structural representation is wrong. This includes combining or splitting words incorrectly, or missing quiet letters entirely (such as dropping the silent "H" in Italian grammar rules).

Phonetic Errors

Difficulties involving the physical attributes of the language's sound system. This commonly displays as omitting required double consonants (doppie) or failing to place mandatory accents on words.

Levelling the Field: Compensatory Solutions

Because dysorthography is an automated processing variation, simply telling a student to "re-read their essay" or grading them down with red ink does not fix the underlying barrier. Instead, it creates anxiety and writing avoidance.

To foster genuine inclusivity and evaluate what the student actually knows rather than their neurological speed bumps, specific modern digital tools are protected by school framework protocols:

  • Advanced Digital Spellcheckers: Real-time text editors that highlight phonological anomalies without penalizing creative flow.
  • Predictive Text Assist: Intelligent keyboards that suggest the correct lexical forms, significantly reducing mental exhaustion.
  • Speech-to-Text Software: Dictation tools that bypass the physical encoding block entirely, allowing students to speak their thoughts directly onto digital paper.
An Inclusivity Perspective: A spelling error is simply a processing hiccup, not a reflection of thought depth. When we use digital tools to look past orthographic bottlenecks, we allow brilliant minds to express their true academic capabilities without restriction.
